Back to Normal—for Which Jobs?

An uneven employment recovery in U.S. leisure and hospitality

Why I looked at this

Hello everyone, welcome back!

In my last post, I looked at how income measurement changes our view of economic mobility. This time, I wanted to explore a related issue: how much can an industry total tell us about the jobs within it?

By December 2025, U.S. leisure and hospitality had slightly more jobs than before the pandemic. But were all three major parts of the industry back to their February 2020 job levels?

Accommodation still had about 8% fewer jobs. That gap made me want to look beyond the total.

What the data measure

I use monthly, seasonally adjusted Bureau of Labor Statistics payroll estimates from FRED for January 2019–December 2025. The three components are arts, entertainment, and recreation; accommodation; and food services and drinking places. The dashed line is their total.

Seasonal adjustment removes recurring seasonal patterns. These series count jobs, not people; someone holding two payroll jobs can appear twice. I use the saved October 26, 2026 download because historical estimates can be revised.

For each component, I divide monthly jobs by February 2020 jobs and multiply by 100. An index of 100 means back to baseline; 105 means 5% above it.

from pathlib import Path
from datetime import date
import requests
import pandas as pd
import plotly.graph_objects as go

raw = Path("data/raw")
processed = Path("data/processed")
figures = Path("results/figures")
tables = Path("results/tables")
for folder in [raw, processed, figures, tables]:
    folder.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)

names = {
    "CES7071000001": "Arts & recreation",
    "CES7072100001": "Accommodation",
    "CES7072200001": "Food & drink services",
    "USLAH": "Industry total"
}
url = "https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id=" + ",".join(names)
csv_file = raw / "fred_employment.csv"
if not csv_file.exists():
    response = requests.get(url, timeout=60)
    response.raise_for_status()
    csv_file.write_bytes(response.content)
    (raw / "download_date.txt").write_text(date.today().isoformat())

jobs = pd.read_csv(csv_file, index_col="observation_date", parse_dates=True)
jobs = jobs.loc["2019-01-01":"2025-12-01", list(names)].rename(columns=names)
assert jobs.index.equals(pd.date_range("2019-01-01", "2025-12-01", freq="MS"))
assert jobs.notna().all().all() and jobs.gt(0).all().all()

baseline = jobs.loc["2020-02-01"]
index = jobs.div(baseline).mul(100)
change = index - 100
millions = jobs / 1000  # FRED reports these series in thousands of jobs.
jobs.to_csv(processed / "monthly_jobs_thousands.csv")
index.to_csv(processed / "monthly_index.csv")

summary = pd.DataFrame({
    "feb_2020_thousands": baseline,
    "dec_2025_thousands": jobs.iloc[-1],
    "change_jobs": (jobs.iloc[-1] - baseline) * 1000,
    "change_percent": change.iloc[-1],
    "baseline_share_percent": baseline / baseline["Industry total"] * 100
})
for sector in jobs.columns:
    later = index.loc["2020-03-01":, sector]
    crossings = later[later >= 100]
    summary.loc[sector, "first_return"] = (
        crossings.index[0].strftime("%Y-%m") if len(crossings) else "Not by Dec 2025"
    )
    summary.loc[sector, "trough_month"] = later.idxmin().strftime("%Y-%m")
    summary.loc[sector, "trough_change_percent"] = later.min() - 100
summary.to_csv(tables / "recovery_summary.csv", index_label="sector")

After the initial drop

The first thing I notice is how similar the initial losses were. At their spring 2020 lows, all three components had lost roughly half their February jobs. Arts and recreation fell the most, about 52%, but was also the first to regain its old level, in May 2023. That’s what I find interesting here: the biggest initial loss didn’t lead to the longest recovery.

You can check the later years for yourself: move the slider to 2023–2025. By December 2025, arts and recreation was 7.4% above baseline. Food and drink services was just 0.5% above, while accommodation remained 8.0% below.

I wouldn’t describe the first two as equally recovered. Food services first crossed 100 in October 2025 in this download, and by December it was still barely above the line. Arts and recreation had more room to spare. The exact crossing month could shift with revisions; the accommodation shortfall is a much clearer feature of this comparison.

Why the total looks better

Now switch to Jobs (millions). Food and drink services is much larger than the other components: it accounted for about 73% of the industry’s jobs in February 2020. That makes its small net gain important for the total. Setting every series to 100 in February 2020 makes their changes easy to compare, but hides that difference in size.

The job counts help put the recovery together. Arts and recreation added about 184,000 jobs between February 2020 and December 2025. Accommodation had about 169,000 fewer. Those gains and losses almost canceled out. Food services added another 58,000, leaving the published total about 72,000 jobs, or 0.4%, above baseline. The small mismatch in the arithmetic comes from source rounding.

This is why I wanted both views in the same chart. In the index view, accommodation’s slower recovery is easy to see. Switching to job counts shows how increases elsewhere were large enough to cover that gap in the total.

Try stopping at September 2025. The industry total is about 18,000 jobs above baseline, even though accommodation is still around 186,000 below and food services is just below its own starting point. Arts and recreation is about 206,000 above, enough to offset those shortfalls. That is the detail I find useful in the animation: we can inspect the mix of gains and losses at the moment the total crosses zero, rather than only compare the final values. The exact crossing month remains sensitive to data revisions.

Takeaway

By December 2025, the industry had regained its overall job count, but accommodation hadn’t. So I think a recovery date needs context: it marks when gains elsewhere offset remaining losses, not when every part of the industry recovered.

For me, the useful lesson is to ask where the jobs came back. A gain in arts and recreation can offset a loss in accommodation in a spreadsheet. It doesn’t tell us whether the people who lost accommodation jobs found comparable work, or whether their pay and hours recovered. We’d need different data to answer those questions.

February 2020 is also only a starting point. Matching it doesn’t tell us how many jobs there would have been without the pandemic. So I’d treat crossing 100 as a milestone, then look at which activities are still behind before calling the recovery complete.
